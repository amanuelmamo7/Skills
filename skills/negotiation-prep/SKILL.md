# Negotiation Prep

Negotiations are won in preparation: the side that knows its walk-away, its target, its alternatives, and the other side's picture of the world makes better decisions under pressure. This skill builds that preparation and keeps it disciplined in the room.

> Born from: mock-negotiation preparation and exercise debriefs from ADR training — the prep sheets and lessons distilled here were used and tested in live exercises. Built with `skill-distiller`.

## Boundary card

**Hard lines** — this skill never:
- Communicates with the counterparty. It preps, drafts talking points, and debriefs; the human negotiates (`references/legal-house-rules.md`, rule 8's analog — support, not substitution).
- Writes the reservation point into anything that could be shared. Prep sheets carry it under a `NEVER DISCLOSE` header; talking-points drafts never contain it. Revealing your walk-away is the one unrecoverable prep error.
- Invents the other side's numbers. Estimates of their economics are labeled estimates with the reasoning shown — a guessed number treated as intelligence produces confident bad strategy.

**Judgment vs. determinism:** what the interests are, how to weigh tradeoffs, when to walk — judgment (the human's). The prep-sheet structure, the never-disclose rules, and the bias checklist are fixed.

## Phase 1 — The prep sheet (`assets/negotiation-prep-template.md`)

1. **Reservation point** — the number/terms below which you walk, and the reasoning (cost floor, alternative deal, risk tolerance). Decided *before* the session, because deciding it during is how escalation of commitment wins.
2. **Target** — the best feasible outcome, with the justification you'd say out loud. An ambitious anchor you can defend beats a safe one; an indefensible one costs credibility.
3. **BATNA and WATNA** — best and worst alternatives to a deal, concretely. Your BATNA is your real leverage; knowing it is also the antidote to both desperation and bluff-calling.
4. **Issue inventory** — every negotiable point, not just price: term, scope, timing, risk allocation, relationship terms. Multi-issue negotiations create trades; single-issue negotiations create standoffs.
5. **Perspective-taking map** — for the other side: why are they at this table, what do they want beyond the obvious, what do they believe about their alternatives (and is it true?), what do they *not* know that shapes their position, what do they think *you* don't know. Their misconceptions are negotiating assets; yours are liabilities — list both.
6. **Information plan** — what to find out before and during (their economics, their constraints, their deadline), what to ask in the room, and the never-disclose list: reservation point, numbers that weaken you, pressure you're under.

## Phase 2 — Strategy

- **Anchoring:** decide first-offer posture deliberately. Letting them open risks a bad anchor that reframes everything after it; opening yourself sets the range but forfeits the pleasant surprise. If they anchor badly, counter-anchor by stating your alternative or demanding a reasonable revision — shrink the range before bargaining inside it.
- **Concession plan:** map increments in advance. Large moves and fast responses read as eagerness and invite pressure; small, slowing increments signal a floor approaching. Every concession asks for something back.
- **Framing moves:** present value in the frame the other side prices (net revenue to them, not cost to you); where the parties price a risk differently, propose terms that trade on the difference (contingent pricing, variable rates); a planned-anyway commitment can be framed as a concession that earns a return.
- **Integrative pass:** before treating the pie as fixed, list trades that expand it — different valuations, timing differences, relationship value separated from dollar value and addressed on its own track (interests, not positions).

## Phase 3 — In the room / debrief

Session discipline: settle each topic fully before moving to the next — parallel open issues are where value leaks; demand point-by-point responses when the other side skips; take a break when stalled rather than conceding to end discomfort; and guard the post-handshake nibble — agreed is agreed, goodwill is not a concession budget. After the session, run the debrief block: what did their concession pattern reveal, what did we learn versus assume, what's the updated map for round two.

**Bias checklist (run before every session and before accepting any deal):** overconfidence in your read · underconfidence bought by their anchor · escalation of commitment past your reservation point · fixed-pie assumption · anchor contamination in your "updated" numbers · frame-induced risk misjudgment. Each is checked against the prep sheet — that's what the sheet is *for*.

## What would make this skill wrong

If negotiations are consistently repeat-player relationships (same counterparty quarterly), the strategy phase underweights relationship capital — add a relationship ledger. If the user's real sessions never follow the prep sheet, the sheet is too heavy for the deal size — make a short-form mode. If a reservation point ever appears in a shared document, the never-disclose mechanism failed structurally — separate the prep sheet into internal and shareable files.
