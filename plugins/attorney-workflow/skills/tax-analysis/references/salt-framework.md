# State & Local Tax — Constitutional Gates

The overlay for "can the state tax this?" questions. Distilled from SALT course materials (nexus case timeline and constitutional case briefs). Federal analysis never answers these questions; run the gates in order.

## Gate 1 — Due Process: may the state reach this taxpayer at all?

Minimum connection ("definite link") between the state and the person, property, or transaction it seeks to tax (*Miller Bros. v. Maryland*, 1954). Post-*Wayfair*, due process and commerce-clause nexus have largely converged in practice, but they remain analytically distinct — a tax can satisfy one and fail the other.

## Gate 2 — Commerce Clause: the *Complete Auto* four-part test (1977)

A state tax on interstate commerce survives only if it:
1. applies to an activity with a **substantial nexus** to the taxing state;
2. is **fairly apportioned** (the state taxes only its share of the activity — internal and external consistency);
3. does **not discriminate** against interstate commerce; and
4. is **fairly related** to services the state provides.

**The nexus line's evolution** (the story to tell when substantiality is contested): *Miller Bros.* (1954, definite-link due process) → *National Bellas Hess* (1967, bright-line physical presence for use-tax collection) → *Complete Auto* (1977, the four-part framework) → *Quill* (1992, physical presence reaffirmed for sales/use collection on stare decisis grounds while due process nexus relaxed) → *South Dakota v. Wayfair* (2018, physical-presence rule overruled; **economic nexus** — substantial virtual/economic presence suffices; statutory thresholds like sales volume or transaction counts now define practical nexus state by state). When advising on a current threshold, the state's own statute is the authority — thresholds vary and change; look them up, never recall them.

## Gate 3 — Discrimination and equality constraints

- **Privileges & Immunities:** discriminatory taxes on nonresidents fall (*Austin v. New Hampshire* — commuter tax effectively borne only by nonresidents).
- **Equal Protection:** favoring in-state over out-of-state business is invalid (*Metropolitan Life v. Ward* — domestic-insurer preference).
- **State uniformity clauses:** many state constitutions demand equality/proportionality/uniformity beyond federal floors (the *Opinion of the Justices* line); acquisition-value property schemes survived federal Equal Protection in *Nordlinger v. Hahn* — state grounds can be stricter than federal.

## Working notes

- **Use tax** complements sales tax: goods bought out-of-state for in-state use; the collection-duty fights (Bellas Hess → Wayfair) are about who must collect, not whether tax is owed.
- Characterize the tax first (income, sales/use, property, gross receipts) — the gates apply differently by type, and apportionment methodology is type-specific.
- Federal statutory overlays can preempt state taxing power in specific domains; check for a controlling federal statute before concluding a state may tax.
- Every conclusion here is federal-constitutional floor analysis; the specific state's statutes, regulations, and cases decide the actual answer — Tier the authorities accordingly.
